Slide 3 — The end of Designing. The Beginning of Curating. - Design Systems today vs Design systems tomorrow

So far design systems were a construction kit about reusability. They solved the problem of having a shared language, tokens, components, patterns — so products could scale without fragmenting. But they were always a construction kit. Someone still decided the layout. Someone still chose the components. Someone still wrote the content for every slot. The output was always a fixed artifact: a page, a screen, a flow — identical for every user who saw it. With AI agents, MCP servers, and composable architecture, that someone's role shifts from maker to curator. And I want to stress: that's not a diminished role, it's an elevated one. Curators define taste, enforce coherence, set boundaries, and make judgment calls that automation can't replicate. The system does the assembly. The designer — and increasingly, the engineer — does the thinking."


So you might ask: what is the problem we are ultimately trying to solve here? Well, design systems have solved the real problem so far. They gave teams a shared language, tokens, components, patterns, and so on to make sure we can scale products in a consistent way without fragmentation. But the problem is the designers still had to do all the manual work of pushing the pixels and moving the boxes and assembling everything manually, with the output being static artifacts. And when you think about the value of a designer, the thinking, the exploration, the discovery of the most valuable user experience, all of that manual lift, heavily think, caused a lot of friction. And what the AI agents and MCPs will do is remove exactly that friction. And that means the role of the designer and product and engineer overall, just like product and engineer overall, is shifting from maker to curator. So now the role is define taste, enforce coherence, set boundaries, make the judgment calls. This is a fundamental shift, and design systems will play an even more important role in it now that the agents are the intermediaries that are doing the assembly, while we, as design, engineering, and product, are doing the thinking and the judgment.


Slide 4 — From Design System to Brand System

"Here's where the shift gets interesting for us as engineers. What we're talking about is a fundamental move from a design system to a brand identity system.

A design system is essentially all about consistency. And consistency is mechanical — same colours, same spacing, same component API, and so on. We can lint for that. We can enforce it in CI.

A brand system is all about meaning. And meaning is contextual — why is this colour here? What emotional cadence does the spacing create? What visceral response does this interaction pattern trigger? That's the part you can't enforce with a linter.

A brand identity system answers the question why — and fundamentally, it tells the brand story at the given moment in the user journey.

And this matters because when an agent is generating UI on the fly, mechanical consistency isn't enough. We need the system itself to carry the reasoning — so that generated experiences don't just look right, they are right."


slide 5

"Concretely, here's the change. Today's design systems are static registries: a set of tokens, a component library, documentation, Figma plugins. They answer the question 'what can we build with it?' Tomorrow's systems answer a fundamentally different question: 'what should we build — for this person, in this moment, on this channel?' That's the jump from a library to an orchestrator. And it's also where the anti-patterns live. If we simply bolt AI onto a static registry, we get faster production of the same fixed artifacts. The real change is making the system queryable, contextual, and opinionated enough that agents can make good decisions with it

Slide 8 — Personalised UI with Nebula MCP and Generated Personas

"So let's move from theory to an actual experiment we ran.

The starting point was two personas — both generated, both structured as data rather than prose. On the left, the Guided Beginner: Sophie, 32, a new investor on execution-only, cautious, low experience. She wants reassurance, plain language, and confidence before she commits money. Her pain points are low confidence in picking funds, jargon, and — importantly — calling customer service for recommendations we legally can't give.

On the right, the Active Comparer: Olivia, 48, an existing investor with an ISA, moderate-to-high risk appetite, medium-to-high experience. She wants to compare funds quickly, make evidence-based decisions, and shortlist before buying. Her pain points are the opposite problem — no effective comparison experience, flipping between tabs and documents, key information sitting outside the buying flow.

Same product. Same fund range. Two people who need fundamentally different experiences. Notice the format: this is JSON. It's machine-readable. That's deliberate — the persona is context the agent can consume, not a poster on a wall."


Slide 9 — The Prompt

"Here's the prompt. One paragraph: use the client personas and the Nebula MCP to build a Vanguard fund page for each individual, personalise it based on their investing experience, and add basket functionality and interactive graphs.

That's it. No component names, no layout instructions, no design spec.

What you see underneath is what happened next — and this is the part I want you to pay attention to. The agent didn't start writing code. It started querying Nebula MCP: fetching component docs for Tag and Card Vertical, searching our icon library for 'bookmark' and 'basket', pulling component details for Widget, Button, AccordionSmall. Then it built the page using only verified Nebula components — you can see it confirming there's no dedicated table component before choosing an alternative.

This is the context layer doing its job. The agent didn't guess what a Vanguard button looks like. It asked.


Slide 10 — The Outcome (the beginner)

"This is what Sophie got.

A guidance-not-advice banner right at the top — which matters, because remember her pain point about seeking recommendations we can't legally provide. 'Find a fund that's right for you', with plain-English explanations. Three funds presented as cards: who this fund is for, risk level clearly labelled, investment objective, and a 'why this fund may suit you' section written in her language — 'because you're just getting started and want reassurance rather than big swings.'

On the right, an illustrative growth chart — £1,000 over ten years — with the appropriate caveat that these are illustrative figures, not a forecast. And a basket, with a note that nothing here is final.

Every element on this page is a Nebula component. Every piece of copy is tuned to her persona. Nobody designed this page. It was assembled from context."
